## Basics first

- **Stated fact.** A fact appears directly in the passage, such as an entry time. Quote or paraphrase the relevant words before inferring from it.
- **Warranted inference.** An inference uses stated facts and their logical consequences. A plausible explanation is only one possibility if the evidence also allows alternatives.
- **Rule application.** A stated rule specifies conditions and an outcome. For an AND rule, check every requirement for the same person and situation before applying the outcome.
- **Causal and scope limits.** Events happening together can have multiple explanations. A result observed in two places at one time may not hold everywhere or on every day.

## Methods and when to use them

### Method A · Separate observations from explanations

Write what the passage recorded and what it left unknown. For a sensor, ask what types of event can trigger it before attributing an observed reading to someone.

**When to use it.** Use this in **Reading Comprehension / Warranted Inference** questions when asked what can be concluded from an observation and the alternatives suggest different possible explanations.

**Key rule.** Separate what was observed from what might explain it. Draw only the inference supported by the text.

#### Type 1 · Inference from timing

**Question.** The gate log shows the last worker left at 6 pm. A light sensor recorded movement at 6:15. The sensor detects animals as well as people. What can be inferred?

**Picture it.** Draw a short timeline with 6:00 and 6:15. The sensor proves something triggered it. Ask what a sensor that can see animals and people can tell apart.

1. **Put the events on a timeline.** The last worker left at 6 pm and the sensor registered movement at 6:15 pm. The record therefore shows a reading 15 minutes after the worker left.
2. **Check what the instrument can distinguish.** The sensor responds to animals and to people. No added clue such as a camera or a second entry log identifies the source.
3. **Limit the inference to what follows.** The log supports the timing and existence of the reading. A returning worker or an animal is possible, so the source remains undetermined.

**Answer:** There was a movement reading after the worker left, and its source is undetermined.

### Method B · Create a condition checklist

Write each requirement in its own slot. Mark which the given case satisfies, fails or leaves unknown. Apply only the rule for the setting asked about.

**When to use it.** Use this in **Reading Comprehension / Applying a Rule** questions when a passage supplies a policy or set of criteria and the question gives a new case to check.

**Key rule.** Break the rule into individual conditions and check each against the new case before deciding where the rule leads.

#### Type 1 · Apply a decision rule

**Question.** To discuss indoors, a group must reserve a room and close its door. Walk-ins can discuss in the courtyard. A group has reserved a room but leaves the door open. Does its discussion meet the indoor rule?

**Picture it.** Give the group two indoor checkboxes, one for booking and one for closing the door. The courtyard rule belongs on a different card.

1. **Write the conditions for indoor discussion.** The rule requires a reservation and a closed door. The word “and” means both conditions must hold for the same group.
2. **Test the conditions separately.** The group meets the reservation requirement. The door is open, so it fails the second requirement.
3. **Apply the rule for the correct location.** Courtyard access concerns a different location. The group can meet the stated indoor rule by closing the room door.

**Answer:** No. The group must also close the room door.

### Method C · Match alternatives to the claim they would challenge

To weaken a causal argument, a competing cause should be comparable in time and affect the same outcome. To reject an overbroad inference, point to a meaningful place, time or population outside the evidence.

**When to use it.** Use this in **Reading Comprehension / Weakening and Scope** questions when an option introduces an alternative cause or tests whether evidence from one setting supports a wider claim.

**Key rule.** A weakening fact must challenge the same claim. A scope objection identifies who, where or when falls outside the evidence.

#### Type 1 · Weaken a causal claim

**Question.** A report says a bicycle-sharing campaign reduced car traffic because car counts fell immediately after launch. Which finding would weaken the claim: a simultaneous bridge closure diverted traffic, or rainy weeks reduced bicycle use in another year?

**Picture it.** Two things happened at once. If a bridge closure could have put the cars on a different road, fewer cars on the counted road need not have come from the bicycle campaign.

1. **Identify the claim and its evidence.** The report credits the campaign for a fall in car counts because that fall followed the launch.
2. **Look for another cause at the same time.** A bridge closure can divert cars out of the counted area and also occurred when the counts fell. With those facts, the timing alone cannot isolate the campaign’s effect.
3. **Compare the two proposed facts.** Rain affecting bicycle use in a different year does not account for the car counts during this campaign. The simultaneous diversion weakens the claim directly.

**Answer:** The simultaneous bridge closure.

#### Type 2 · Scope of a conclusion

**Question.** Two shaded bus stops were cooler at noon than adjacent unshaded stops on a clear day. Can you infer shaded stops are cooler under every condition?

**Picture it.** Imagine checking the two pairs of stops on this one clear day. A different day or a different stop would need its own check.

1. **Mark the observations the passage gives.** Only two shaded stops were compared with nearby unshaded ones, at noon on a clear day.
2. **Check how far the claim goes.** “Every condition” includes other stops, times, seasons and weather. The passage supplies no comparisons under those conditions.
3. **State the narrower inference.** The shaded stops were cooler in the observed noon comparison. Other conditions would need their own observations.

**Answer:** Only the observed noon comparison is supported.

## Quick revision sheet

| When you see | Use this rule |
| --- | --- |
| “Can be inferred” | Choose a claim forced or best supported by the supplied facts. |
| Requires A and B | Verify both A and B before applying the rule. |
| Weakens claimed causation | Find another plausible cause of the same outcome during the same interval. |
| Claim says “under every condition” | Check whether the observations cover those conditions. |

## Exam tips

- **Do not treat “if” as “only if”.** Use the direction actually stated. “Must” explicitly sets a requirement.
- **Keep uncertainty when it matters.** A reading can be certain while its source remains unknown.
- **Match the scope of evidence.** An event in another year usually cannot explain counts from the period under study.
