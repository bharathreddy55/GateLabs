## Basics first

- **Evidence.** Evidence is a stated fact or observation. Identify which information the author actually supplied.
- **Conclusion.** The conclusion is what the evidence is meant to support. It may be a recommendation, explanation or prediction.
- **Necessary assumption.** An assumption supplies a bridge from evidence to conclusion. If it is necessary, denying it disrupts the stated reasoning.
- **Alternatives.** A rival cause or an unrepresentative sample may produce the same observation. This raises a distinct issue from whether the plan sounds appealing.

## Methods and when to use them

### Method A · Reduce the argument to evidence and goal

Write “because … therefore …” in your own words. Ask which condition must connect those two statements. For a new counter to affect a queue, it must serve at least part of that queue.

### Method B · Negate the proposed assumption

Negate “some can be helped” to “none can be helped.” If the plan no longer has a mechanism, the assumption is necessary. Negating “every worker prefers evenings” permits some who prefer evenings, so the plan can still work.

### Method C · Match the missing link to the argument type

A survey needs a justified link from respondents to the stated population. A causal claim based on timing needs a reason to rule out an alternative that fully explains the effect. A plan needs a mechanism tying action to outcome.

## Question models and worked examples

### Plan and feasibility

**Question.** A station will open an extra staffed counter to shorten queues. What does the plan assume about capacity?

**Picture it.** Imagine the new counter opens but cannot help anyone already in the queue. Ask whether it could shorten that queue. This shows what the plan depends on.

1. **Identify the intended effect.** The station adds a staffed counter to reduce time waiting. The plan assumes the counter contributes service capacity relevant to that queue.
2. **Test the missing link.** If the counter serves nobody already waiting or joining that queue, it adds no capacity for those customers.
3. **State the necessary assumption.** The counter can serve at least some customers from that queue. The plan can still work even if it serves only part of the demand.

**Answer:** The extra counter can serve at least some customers in the queue.

### Unnecessary absolute

**Question.** A library extends evening hours to reach more workers. Must it assume every worker prefers evening visits?

**Picture it.** An evening library can help people who can only visit later. It does not need everyone to want the same opening time.

1. **Identify the goal.** Extending evening hours aims to reach additional workers who would use the library at that time.
2. **Test the universal statement.** Suppose one worker would rather visit during the day. Evening hours can still reach other workers who need them.
3. **State the weaker premise.** The plan needs demand among at least some workers. It can work with mixed preferences.

**Answer:** No. The plan needs some relevant demand, not universal preference.

### Representativeness

**Question.** A survey of only monthly-pass holders claims most station users want a changed timetable. What assumption bridges the sample and claim?

**Picture it.** Think of asking one class what the entire school likes. Their answers can represent the school only if that class is a good guide to everyone else.

1. **Identify the sample and the target.** The survey records monthly-pass holders’ preferences. The conclusion applies to all station users, including people without passes.
2. **Find the missing link.** Pass holders may have different schedules or priorities. To generalise, their answers must reflect the wider population on this question.
3. **Test the assumption’s role.** If pass holders favour a change while most other users oppose it, the stated evidence would no longer support the station-wide claim.

**Answer:** The sample is representative of all users’ timetable preferences.

### Causal alternative

**Question.** Delays fell after a new dispatch procedure. A report attributes the reduction to that procedure. What assumption is essential to the inference from timing alone?

**Picture it.** Picture a new rule and new equipment arriving on the same day. If the equipment can explain all the improvement, the timing does not tell us the rule did it.

1. **Separate the observation from the claim.** The report observes delays falling after the change, then credits the procedure. A before-and-after sequence alone leaves the cause open.
2. **Try a rival explanation.** If a route closure ended at the same time and fully explained the improvement, the observed fall could occur without a benefit from the new procedure.
3. **Identify the necessary bridge.** The inference requires that an unrelated change does not fully account for the fall. Evidence of other factors would be needed to test the claim.

**Answer:** No unrelated change fully accounts for the observed fall.

## Quick revision sheet

| When you see | Use this rule |
| --- | --- |
| Plan to reduce a problem | Look for minimum feasibility of the proposed mechanism. |
| Sample to whole population | Check whether the sample represents the target on the measured issue. |
| Change follows an intervention | Ask whether a concurrent cause can explain the same change. |
| Candidate assumption is an absolute | Try a weaker condition that still lets the argument work. |

## Exam tips

- **Distinguish support and necessity.** Something helpful may still be optional to the argument.
- **Keep the strength of the claim.** “Some” and “all” require different justification.
- **Test relevance to the conclusion.** A true statement about the station may have no bearing on the inference.
