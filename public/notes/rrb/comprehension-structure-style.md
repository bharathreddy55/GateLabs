## Basics first

- **Logical structure.** Structure is the order of the argument’s jobs. A passage may state a claim, support it, handle an objection and recommend an action.
- **Signal phrases.** “However” often introduces a contrast. “For example” points to an illustration. “Therefore” signals a conclusion, and “should” may introduce a recommendation.
- **Tone.** Tone is how the author handles the subject. Hedging words like “may” and “suggest” indicate limited confidence, especially when paired with requests for more evidence.
- **Example and counterargument.** An example makes a general point concrete. A counterargument presents an objection that the author may accept in part, answer or use to revise the claim.

## Methods and when to use them

### Method A · Give each sentence a role

Read it in sequence and label its job with one phrase, such as benefit, limitation or action. If an option swaps the order or invents missing evidence, compare it to your labels.

**When to use it.** Use this in **Reading Comprehension / Organisation** questions when asked how a sentence or paragraph develops the argument, such as by adding evidence, a contrast or a next step.

**Key rule.** Label the role of each relevant sentence, such as benefit, contrast, limitation or conclusion, in the order it appears.

#### Type 1 · Concession followed by judgment

**Question.** “Automatic announcements are consistent. However, during disruptions they can lag behind decisions made on the platform. A manual update should remain available.” How is the argument organised?

**Picture it.** Give each sentence a job. The first says what works, the next shows when it has trouble and the last tells what to keep.

1. **Read the first sentence as a benefit.** The passage begins by recognising consistency as a strength of automatic announcements.
2. **Follow the shift signalled by “however”.** The disruption example introduces a specific limitation. Automated updates can trail decisions already made on the platform.
3. **Find the proposed action.** “Should remain available” recommends keeping a manual option to address that limitation. This gives benefit, limitation, then recommendation.

**Answer:** Benefit, limitation, then recommendation.

### Method B · Cite two clues for tone

Pick one word about confidence and one about the author’s approach. “May” signals caution. Comparing readings and requesting more data shows an analytical approach.

**When to use it.** Use this in **Reading Comprehension / Tone and Attitude** questions when asked how the author feels about a claim or how strongly the author expresses it.

**Key rule.** Use wording about certainty and the author’s treatment of evidence together to identify tone.

#### Type 1 · Infer tone from qualifiers

**Question.** “The data suggest the repair may have reduced noise, though readings from additional sites are needed.” What is the tone?

**Picture it.** Listen to the words may and suggest. The writer sounds like someone who has seen a clue and wants more clues before making a firm claim.

1. **Identify words that qualify the claim.** The author says the data “suggest” that the repair “may” have reduced noise. Both words leave room for another outcome.
2. **Read the condition on confidence.** The author asks for readings from more sites, showing what evidence would help test the tentative result.
3. **Connect wording to tone.** The emphasis on readings makes the tone analytical. The qualifiers and request for additional evidence make it cautious.

**Answer:** Cautious and analytical.

### Method C · Ask what changes if the sentence is removed

If only the concrete case disappears while the main claim survives, the sentence illustrates. If an objection and reply disappear together, the paragraph was addressing resistance to a claim.

**When to use it.** Use this in **Reading Comprehension / Purpose of an Example or Objection** questions when asked why the author included a particular example, comparison or objection.

**Key rule.** Imagine removing the sentence or paragraph and ask whether the passage loses an example, objection, reply or other part of its reasoning.

#### Type 1 · Function of a specific example

**Question.** “Small inefficiencies accumulate over a timetable. A two-minute turnaround delay repeated across six departures costs twelve minutes.” Why is the second sentence included?

**Picture it.** Picture one tiny delay being copied six times. That number gives you a real-sized example of the bigger idea.

1. **Find the general statement.** The first sentence says that small inefficiencies can add up across a timetable.
2. **Trace the arithmetic in the example.** A two-minute delay happens six times. Multiplying 2 by 6 gives 12 minutes of accumulated delay in the example.\
   `2 min × 6 departures = 12 min`
3. **Name the sentence’s role.** The calculation turns the general idea into a concrete case the reader can check. It illustrates the claim.

**Answer:** To illustrate the claim with a numerical example.

#### Type 2 · Role of a counterargument

**Question.** A passage argues for reusing older station buildings. Its second paragraph notes that adapting them for accessibility can be costly, then describes phased upgrades and how these can spread the cost. What is that paragraph doing?

**Picture it.** Think of someone saying the old building costs too much to fix. The writer explains that worry and then shows a way to spread the cost.

1. **Find the author’s position.** The passage supports reusing older station buildings.
2. **Separate the objection from the reply.** The second paragraph concedes that accessibility work can be expensive. It then presents phased upgrades as a way to spread the cost over time.
3. **Read the paragraph as one argument.** The cost concern is acknowledged, then addressed with a practical response. The paragraph supports the larger case by showing how to handle an objection.

**Answer:** Acknowledging an objection and responding to it.

## Quick revision sheet

| When you see | Use this rule |
| --- | --- |
| Benefit then “however” then “should” | Acknowledgement, limitation, recommendation. |
| “Data suggest” and “more readings are needed” | A tentative, analytical judgment. |
| Generalisation followed by a calculation | Usually an illustration if the calculation shows the general pattern. |
| Objection followed by a mitigation | Concession followed by a response. |

## Exam tips

- **Distinguish topic from sentence function.** A sentence about cost can present an objection rather than state the passage’s main subject.
- **Anchor tone in the author’s words.** The issue may be emotional even when the wording is neutral.
- **Preserve the order in structure answers.** A recommendation at the end is different from one introduced then defended.
