## Basics first

- **Work.** A force transfers energy when it has a component along a displacement. A constant force F makes angle θ with movement, so its work is Fs cos θ.
- **Signed contributions.** Force along movement gives positive work. Opposing force gives negative work. Perpendicular forces contribute zero work.
- **Energy.** Kinetic energy is ½mv². Near Earth in uniform g, lifting an object by h increases gravitational potential energy by mgh.
- **Power and efficiency.** Average power is work or energy transferred per unit time. Efficiency is useful output divided by total input and has no units.

## Methods and when to use them

### Method A · Project and add forces separately

An angled pull contributes its component along the path. Draw friction opposite sliding and assign the sign directly. Sum individual works to get net work.

### Method B · Use the change in kinetic energy

If mass and speeds are supplied, compare the kinetic energy before and after. This gives net work even if the separate forces are unknown. Constant speed means zero change in kinetic energy.

### Method C · Separate input and output

For a motor lifting steadily, useful work is mgh. Divide by time for useful output power. Divide that output by efficiency as a decimal to recover the larger input.

## Question models and worked examples

### Oblique pull against friction

**Question.** Pull an object 10 m horizontally with 50 N at 60° above horizontal. Friction is 8 N. Find the net work if the other forces are vertical.

1. **Project the pull along the motion.** Only the component parallel to the 10 m displacement contributes. It is 50 × cos 60° = 25 N, giving work +25 × 10 = +250 J.\
   `50 × cos 60° = 25 N along motion`
2. **Include the opposing force.** Friction points against the displacement, so it contributes −8 × 10 = −80 J. Forces perpendicular to the displacement contribute 0 J.\
   `friction component = −8 N`
3. **Add the signed work.** Net work is the sum from all forces, 250 − 80 + 0 = 170 J.\
   `(25 − 8) × 10 = 170 J`

**Answer:** 170 J.

### Kinetic energy from net work

**Question.** A 4 kg cart speeds up from 3 to 7 m/s. Find the net work.

1. **Find the initial energy.** Use K = ½mv². At 3 m/s, the 4 kg cart has ½ × 4 × 3² = 18 J.\
   `½ × 4 × 3² = 18 J`
2. **Find the final energy.** At 7 m/s, its kinetic energy is ½ × 4 × 7² = 98 J.\
   `½ × 4 × 7² = 98 J`
3. **Apply the work–energy theorem.** Net work equals final kinetic energy minus initial kinetic energy. Here 98 − 18 = 80 J.\
   `98 − 18 = 80 J`

**Answer:** 80 J.

### Lift, power and efficiency

**Question.** A motor raises 60 kg through 8 m in 12 s at constant speed. Use g=10 m/s² and efficiency 80%. Find input power.

1. **Find the useful work.** The load rises at constant speed, so useful work is the increase in potential energy. mgh = 60 × 10 × 8 = 4,800 J.\
   `60 × 10 × 8 = 4,800 J`
2. **Find the output power.** Power is work ÷ time. In 12 s, the motor delivers 4,800 ÷ 12 = 400 W of useful power.\
   `4,800 ÷ 12 = 400 W`
3. **Use efficiency to recover input.** Efficiency is output power ÷ input power. At 80%, 0.80 × input = 400, so input is 400 ÷ 0.80 = 500 W.\
   `400 ÷ 0.80 = 500 W`

**Answer:** 500 W input.

### Constant speed and signed work

**Question.** A crate slides 6 m at steady speed with a 25 N horizontal push and 25 N friction. Find work by each force and net work.

1. **Calculate the push work.** The push and displacement point the same way, giving +25 × 6 = +150 J.\
   `+25 × 6 = +150 J`
2. **Calculate the friction work.** Friction points opposite the motion, giving −25 × 6 = −150 J. Vertical forces, if included, do zero work on the horizontal trip.\
   `−25 × 6 = −150 J`
3. **Check the total.** The two contributions cancel, giving 0 J. This agrees with steady speed, since the change in kinetic energy is zero.\
   `150 − 150 = 0 J`

**Answer:** Push +150 J, friction −150 J, net 0 J.

### Electrical energy use

**Question.** A device drawing 1.2 kW runs for 30 minutes. Give the energy drawn in kWh and joules.

1. **Convert the running time.** Power is stated in kW, so use hours to obtain kWh. Thirty minutes is 0.5 hour.\
   `30 min = 0.5 h`
2. **Multiply by the power.** Energy drawn is 1.2 kW × 0.5 h = 0.6 kWh.\
   `1.2 × 0.5 = 0.6 kWh`
3. **Convert to joules.** 1 kWh = 1,000 J/s × 3,600 s = 3.6 million J. Multiply by 0.6 to get 2,160,000 J.\
   `0.6 × 3.6 MJ = 2.16 MJ`

**Answer:** 0.6 kWh or 2.16 MJ.

## Exam tips

- **Check angles against motion.** Only the angle from force to displacement belongs in cos θ.
- **Keep rate and energy units separate.** Watts are joules each second, and kWh is a quantity of energy.
- **Retain the signs until the sum.** Equal positive and negative works cancel even when both forces transfer energy.
