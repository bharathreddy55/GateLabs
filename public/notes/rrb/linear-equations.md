## Basics first

- **Equation.** An equation says two expressions have the same value. Isolate the unknown by applying the same reversible operation to both sides.
- **Linear equation.** The unknown appears to power one and is multiplied by a constant. A fixed coefficient times the number of tickets, workers or years often gives a linear model.
- **Simultaneous equations.** Each independent equation supplies a different constraint. Two compatible independent linear equations in two unknowns locate one solution.
- **Dependent or inconsistent conditions.** If one equation is a multiple of the other including the constant, they have the same solutions. If their variable terms are proportional and constants disagree under that same scaling, there is no common solution.

## Methods and when to use them

### Method A · Define variables in one time frame

Name present ages before translating the past or future. Subtract or add years to each person’s age. Apply expressions such as twice to the whole relevant age.

**When to use it.** Use this in **Linear Equations / Age Problems** questions when a statement compares ages now, a few years ago or several years from now.

**Key rule.** Age t years from now = present age + t. Use a negative t for the past.

#### Type 1 · Ages across years

**Question.** A is 8 years older than B. Four years ago A was twice B’s age. Find their present ages.

**Picture it.** Give each person an age card. To go back four years, remove four from both cards before using the clue about twice the age.

1. **Name the current ages.** Let B be x years old, so A is x + 8. Four years ago, B was x − 4 and A was x + 4.\
   `B = x and A = x + 8`
2. **Translate “twice” into an equation.** The age condition gives x + 4 = 2(x − 4). Expanding yields x + 4 = 2x − 8, so x = 12.\
   `(x + 8) − 4 = 2(x − 4) ⇒ x + 4 = 2x − 8 ⇒ x = 12`
3. **Check both clues.** A is 12 + 8 = 20. Four years ago they were 16 and 8, and 16 is twice 8.\
   `A = 20, B = 12`\
   `20 − 4 = 16 = 2 × (12 − 4)`

**Answer:** A is 20, B is 12.

### Method B · Use the total to remove one variable

If x of 54 tickets are expensive, the cheaper count is 54−x. Multiply each count by its price and use revenue to solve. Alternatively, compare actual revenue to the cost of pricing every ticket at the cheaper rate.

**When to use it.** Use this in **Linear Equations / Tickets and Prices** questions when the number of items and total bill are known but the split between two prices is unknown.

**Key rule.** If x items have higher price H and the remaining N − x cost L, total revenue = Hx + L(N − x).

#### Type 1 · Ticket count and price

**Question.** 54 tickets at ₹30 or ₹50 bring in ₹2,060. How many of each?

**Picture it.** Pretend every ticket starts as a cheap ticket. Each time you turn one into an expensive ticket, the cash goes up by the same amount.

1. **Choose one unknown.** Let y be the number of ₹50 tickets. That leaves 54 − y tickets at ₹30.\
   `₹50 tickets: x. ₹30 tickets: 54 − x.`
2. **Use the revenue condition.** The two amounts add to 30(54 − y) + 50y = 2,060. Expanding gives 1,620 + 20y = 2,060.\
   `30(54−x) + 50x = 2,060 ⇒ 20x = 440`
3. **Solve and verify.** 20y = 440, so y = 22 and 54 − y = 32. Revenue is 22 × ₹50 + 32 × ₹30 = ₹2,060.\
   `x = 22 and 54 − x = 32`\
   `22 × ₹50 + 32 × ₹30 = ₹2,060`

**Answer:** 32 at ₹30 and 22 at ₹50.

### Method C · Clear fractions and check the equality

Multiply the entire equation by the smallest common denominator. Distribute before bringing like terms together. Substitute into the original to catch a sign or omitted term.

**When to use it.** Use this in **Linear Equations / Fractional Coefficients** questions when the unknown appears in terms with different denominators.

**Key rule.** Multiply each term on both sides by the common denominator. After simplifying, an identity can leave multiple solutions, while a contradiction leaves none.

#### Type 1 · Clear fractions

**Question.** Solve (x−2)/3+(x+1)/4=7.

**Picture it.** The two fractions cut the unknown into thirds and quarters. Multiplying every term by 12 lets you work with whole pieces.

1. **Clear the two denominators.** The least common multiple of 3 and 4 is 12. Multiply every term by 12 to obtain 4(x−2) + 3(x+1) = 84.\
   `4(x − 2) + 3(x + 1) = 84`
2. **Expand and collect.** The left side is 4x − 8 + 3x + 3 = 7x − 5. Therefore 7x = 89.\
   `4x − 8 + 3x + 3 = 84 ⇒ 7x − 5 = 84`
3. **Divide to solve.** x = 89/7. Substitution gives (25/7) + (24/7) = 49/7 = 7 in the original equation.\
   `x = 89/7`

**Answer:** 89/7.

#### Type 2 · Dependent equations

**Question.** Do x+y=12 and 3x+3y=36 determine x and y uniquely?

**Picture it.** Imagine writing the same instruction twice, once in single pieces and once in groups of three. There are still many pairs that add to 12.

1. **Compare the two equations.** Dividing every term of 3x + 3y = 36 by 3 gives x + y = 12, the first equation again.\
   `(3x + 3y = 36) ÷ 3 yields x + y = 12`
2. **Describe the possible pairs.** Choose any real x, and set y = 12 − x. This gives a pair satisfying both equations.\
   `y = 12 − x`
3. **Check that multiple pairs qualify.** For example, (0,12) and (5,7) both work. The second equation supplies no independent constraint.\
   `(0, 12) and (7, 5) both satisfy the two equations`

**Answer:** No. There are infinitely many real solutions.

## Quick revision sheet

| When you see | Use this rule |
| --- | --- |
| Total count N and one count x | Other count = N − x. |
| Each unit has a fixed price | Revenue = sum of quantity × price. |
| Fractional coefficients | Multiply every term on both sides by the common denominator. |
| Two scaled equations | Equal after scaling gives infinitely many solutions for a nontrivial line. Unequal constants give none. |

## Exam tips

- **Define the unit.** For prices in rupees and counts in tickets, the final count must be an integer in range.
- **Keep brackets until distributing.** A multiplier applies to every term inside the bracket.
- **Check every given condition.** A pair that satisfies one equation may fail the other.
