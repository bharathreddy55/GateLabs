Jainism belongs to the broader renunciant traditions that flourished in ancient northern India. In Jain tradition, Mahavira is the twenty-fourth Tirthankara and Parshvanatha the twenty-third. Jain teaching describes living beings as possessing jiva, which can become bound by karmic matter through actions and passions. Liberation requires halting new karmic bondage and shedding accumulated karma through disciplined conduct.

The Three Jewels combine right faith, right knowledge and right conduct. Five vows guide conduct: nonviolence, truthfulness, non-stealing, celibacy and non-possession. Monastics observe the great vows strictly, and householders typically practise adapted vows. Anekantavada recognizes that a complex thing can be described from more than one standpoint.

## Worked example

A question describes travellers avoiding unnecessary injury to insects. The relevant vow is ahimsa, or nonviolence. Giving up excessive possessions illustrates aparigraha, and telling the truth illustrates satya.

## Common mistake

Mahavira is traditionally the last Tirthankara of the present series. Earlier Tirthankaras are part of Jain religious tradition.

## Quick revision

- Mahavira → twenty-fourth Tirthankara.
- Three Jewels → right faith, knowledge and conduct.
- Ahimsa → nonviolence. Aparigraha → non-possession.

## Practice

Which principle helps explain why competing descriptions can each capture aspects of one object? **Answer:** Anekantavada, which considers multiple standpoints.

Why do monastic and household vows differ in strictness? **Answer:** Their obligations differ. The adapted household vows provide a disciplined path within ordinary work and family life.
