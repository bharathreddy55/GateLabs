The crust is Earth's outer chemical layer. The mantle extends from below the crust to roughly 2,900 km depth. The iron-rich core comprises a liquid outer core and a solid inner core. In mechanical terms, the lithosphere includes the crust and rigid uppermost mantle, while the asthenosphere below it can deform and flow over geological timescales.

P waves compress and expand material and can travel through solids and liquids. S waves shear material and travel through solids. Travel times, reflection, refraction and shadow zones show where seismic waves encounter abrupt changes in material. The Mohorovičić discontinuity separates crust from mantle. Plate boundaries explain broad earthquake and volcanic patterns, while some volcanoes occur inside plates.

## Concepts in depth

Source evidence includes seismic waves, gravity and material studies. P waves travel through solids and fluids. S waves propagate through solids and need shear rigidity. Velocity changes and shadow zones help infer layer boundaries. The crust is not identical to the entire lithosphere, which includes rigid upper mantle.

## How to apply it

The liquid outer core explains S-wave shadowing. Distinguish composition-based layers (crust, mantle, core) from mechanical layers (lithosphere, asthenosphere and others). At boundaries, seismic response depends on material properties as well as density.

## Worked example

Seismometers detect no direct S waves along paths through the outer core. Because liquid cannot sustain shear waves, the observation supports a liquid outer core.

## Common mistake

The crust and lithosphere describe different boundaries. The lithosphere includes some upper mantle.

## Practice

Why do P waves still provide information about the liquid outer core?

**Answer:** They can travel through liquids, and their changes in speed and direction constrain the boundary and material properties.
