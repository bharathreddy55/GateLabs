A two-sided limit exists when the left and right limits agree. Continuity at a requires f(a) equal that limit. Differentiability additionally needs a finite common derivative at a and implies continuity. Check denominator restrictions before cancellation, and inspect piecewise boundaries from both sides.

## Concepts in depth

A finite two-sided limit exists if the one-sided limits agree. Continuity also requires the limit to equal the function value. Differentiability implies continuity, and requires a common finite local slope. Cancelling a factor can simplify a limit without changing the domain of the original expression.

## How to apply it

For the absolute-value function at x=0, both function limits agree and equal zero, but the left derivative is −1 and the right is +1. For (x²−1)/(x−1), the limit at 1 equals 2 even though the original expression is undefined there.

## Worked example

For x≠1, (x²−1)/(x−1)=x+1. The limit at 1 is 2 and defining the value as 2 makes the function continuous there.

## Common mistake

Continuity alone does not establish a derivative. A corner has different one-sided slopes.

## Practice

Is |x| differentiable at zero?

**Answer:** No. The left derivative is −1 and the right derivative is 1.
